TAKE ALL COMERS
In Ontario, private insurers may not decline auto insurance to a consumer unless the regulator has approved the ground in advance. This is known as Take All Comers. Its legal basis is the Insurance Act (R.S.O. 1990, c. I.8). The purpose is to keep mandatory auto insurance broadly available. Auto insurance is compulsory to drive in Ontario under the Compulsory Automobile Insurance Act, so being declined has real consequences for a consumer.

Under Take All Comers, an insurer must quote and accept every consumer who meets its approved underwriting rules. An insurer cannot invent a new reason to refuse coverage on the spot. It must file the rule with the regulator and have it approved first.

WHAT AN UNDERWRITING DECLINE RULE IS
An underwriting decline rule is an insurer's right to decline an auto insurance policy based on criteria the regulator has approved. It is an approved exception to Take All Comers. Example: "Decline to insure any applicant with 2 or more at-fault accidents in the preceding 3 years."

A decline rule usually has:
- a trigger: the event or characteristic that causes the decline (for example, at-fault accidents, a fraud conviction, commercial use of the vehicle);
- often a threshold: how many events (for example, 2 or more);
- often a lookback period: how far back the insurer looks (for example, the preceding 3 years);
- a rationale: why the insurer believes the trigger relates to risk.

DECLINE RULES VERSUS ELIGIBILITY RULES
- Decline rule (in scope for Simrule): declines the whole policy.
- Eligibility rule (out of scope): sets criteria for optional coverages or limits. Not meeting them means the consumer cannot buy that optional coverage or limit, but can still buy the policy.

APPROVED RULES ARE NOT APPROVED FOREVER
Underwriting rules differ by insurer but are often similar. Three points from the framework matter:
1. A rule that already exists at another insurer is a consideration, not a guarantee of approval.
2. An approved rule is not approved forever. Market changes and public policy can change what is allowed.
3. The regulator's principles and fair consumer outcomes set a higher fairness standard, so past practice may not be allowed in future.

In Simrule, a match with an approved precedent is recorded with the issue code PRECEDENT_APPROVED_MATCH at Level 1. It helps the rule, but the reviewer still checks Levels 2 to 4, and the insurer is still asked for supporting analysis.

COMMON APPROVED DECLINE RULES (FROM THE FRAMEWORK)
These are examples of common approved practice:
- Use of a personal vehicle for business purposes (the consumer needs commercial insurance).
- Part-time use of a vehicle for package delivery.
- Declining any rebuilt vehicle.
- A pattern of at-fault accidents and tickets over a 3-year period.
- A combination of at-fault accidents or tickets over a short period and cancellations for missed payments.
- A previous fraud conviction.

MORE EXAMPLES OF APPROVED RULES (FROM THE FRAMEWORK)
- 1 or more Criminal Code convictions in the preceding 3 years.
- 2 or more at-fault accidents in the preceding 3 years.
- 2 minor convictions within 3 years and 2 cancellations for non-payment of premium within 3 years.
- 1 at-fault accident in 5 years, 1 minor conviction within 3 years, and 2 cancellations for non-payment within 3 years.
- 1 or more convictions for auto insurance fraud in the preceding 10 years.
- Any automobile used for commercial purposes.
- Used on a racetrack, or in any race, speed test or competition.
- Vehicles used for a purpose for which they were not designed.
- No driver of the insured vehicle holds a valid Canadian licence.
- Vehicles used for police work.
- Failure to pay outstanding premiums owed to the insurer or its affiliates for the same named insured.
- Improper class of licence or invalid licence.
- Any vehicle modified for speed or used for racing.
- Any vehicle with unacceptable customizations or modifications.
In Simrule these appear in the precedent registry as AP-01 to AP-14 with invented insurer names and illustrative approval years.

ILLUSTRATIVE NON-COMPLIANT EXAMPLES (FROM THE FRAMEWORK)
- Declining someone because they have never had insurance before. Fails basic compliance and blocks new drivers from mandatory coverage.
- Declining anyone whose vehicle is one of the three most stolen models in Ontario. Not aligned with the principles; theft risk is better handled through rating or anti-theft requirements.
- Declining people who drive red cars because they are "likely to drive faster". Subjective and arbitrary, with no direct relationship to risk.
- Declining someone accused of a crime they were not convicted of. Relies on an accusation, not a conviction.
- Declining someone who changed insurers more than twice in 10 years. Arbitrary; switching insurers is normal consumer behaviour.

WHAT HAPPENS TO A DECLINED CONSUMER
Because auto insurance is compulsory, a declined consumer must find another insurer or turn to the residual market (the Facility Association), usually at a higher cost, or stop driving. That is why the framework asks whether a rule unfairly limits access to coverage, and why Simrule shows a consumer lens on every rule.
